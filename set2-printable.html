Set 2 explained answers

References are printed textbook pages; add 25 for the supplied PDF viewer. Fictional scenarios and numerical examples are original unless explicitly identified as textbook cases.

1. B — Whether average service-processing time decreases after adoption

Business results concern the value the project was intended to create. Processing-time improvement measures the service outcome; meetings, templates, and activity lists measure management practices or preparation. Set success criteria early and arrange to measure benefits after delivery.

Chapter 1 · Defining success before delivery · printed pp. 15–18

2. B — Schedule

The decisive detail is that the shipment arrives after the required installation date, directly threatening schedule. It may later create cost or other effects, but those are not established by the scenario. Distinguish a stated impact from a possible consequence.

Chapter 1 · Identifying the constrained objective · printed pp. 7–9

3. A — Project communications management

Communications management addresses the generation, distribution, storage, and use of project information. Resource management concerns people and physical resources; procurement concerns outside purchases; quality concerns meeting relevant requirements. The dashboard’s purpose here is information delivery.

Chapter 1 · Knowledge areas in use · printed pp. 10–12

4. B — Project quality management

Including the agreed fields addresses scope coverage; calculating them correctly is a quality concern. Scope and quality interact, but the problem described is a deliverable that fails a correctness requirement.

Chapter 1 · Quality versus scope · printed pp. 10–12

5. C — Gantt chart

A Gantt chart displays activities against time. A WBS dictionary describes components of scope, a weighted scoring model compares alternatives, and stakeholder analysis examines people or groups. Choose the tool that answers the requested question.

Chapter 1 · Selecting appropriate tools · printed pp. 12–15

6. C — Core

Core projects support running the business and the example treats their costs as nondiscretionary. Growth projects expand the business; venture projects transform it. Program describes coordinated related work, not one of these three investment categories.

Chapter 1 · Core, growth, and venture investments · printed pp. 23

7. B — Growth

The textbook’s growth category includes projects intended to increase revenues, such as extending a website to another language market. This is a temporary expansion project; the fact that it modifies an existing system does not make it recurring operations.

Chapter 1 · Growth investments · printed pp. 23

8. A — Choose a combination of investments that best serves strategy within available resources

Portfolio management selects and balances investments against strategic objectives, risk, and resource constraints. The other choices concern managing or delivering work within a particular project. A positive individual case does not eliminate the need to prioritize.

Chapter 1 · Strategic portfolio decisions · printed pp. 19–23

9. A — Project management office

A PMO supports project management across an organization through functions such as standards, training, information, and coordination. Its exact authority varies. A change control board focuses on authorized change decisions, rather than automatically owning all these organizational services.

Chapter 1 · Project management office · printed pp. 32–34

10. D — Leadership through vision and influence

The chapter contrasts leadership’s vision, long-term direction, and inspiration with management’s attention to organizing and controlling specific work. Effective project managers need both; the distinction does not mean one role is unnecessary.

Chapter 1 · Leadership and management · printed pp. 28–29

11. D — Transactional

Transactional leadership emphasizes exchanges, rewards or consequences, and management by exception. Transformational leadership emphasizes inspiring and empowering change. Both can occur in practice, but the described mechanism is the exchange tied to performance.

Chapter 1 · Transactional and transformational leadership · printed pp. 28

12. D — Develop enough understanding to ask good questions and rely on qualified specialists while coordinating the whole project

The PM needs relevant technical understanding, business awareness, and people skills. They do not need to be the top technical expert, but must organize expertise, understand implications, and manage the overall project rather than ignoring technical or stakeholder concerns.

Chapter 1 · Technical competence and delegation · printed pp. 26–27

13. A — True

Fairness, honesty, and responsibility support identifying and addressing conflicts of interest. Apparent technical suitability does not remove a personal interest or justify concealing it.

Chapter 1 · Professional ethics and conflicts · printed pp. 36–37

14. B — False

Portfolios group investments to achieve strategic objectives; their components need not be directly related. Related projects managed together for additional benefits are characteristic of a program.

Chapter 1 · Portfolio components · printed pp. 21–22

15. A — True

Uncertainty is a project attribute. Good management makes assumptions visible, improves estimates as information develops, and controls resulting changes rather than pretending that early knowledge is perfect.

Chapter 1 · Uncertainty and estimates · printed pp. 6–9

16. B — False

A megaproject describes a very large project. A program coordinates related projects and other program activities to gain benefits unavailable through separate management. Size alone does not establish that structure.

Chapter 1 · Megaprojects and programs · printed pp. 18–19

17. D — Systems analysis

Systems analysis is a problem-solving approach that examines the system and alternatives. Systems management addresses business, organizational, and technological issues; systems philosophy supplies the holistic viewpoint. These terms are related but not interchangeable.

Chapter 2 · Systems analysis · printed pp. 49–50

18. A — Business

The business sphere addresses financial justification and contribution to business needs. A tool can be technologically feasible yet poorly justified economically. Symbolic is an organizational frame, not one of the three spheres.

Chapter 2 · Business sphere · printed pp. 49–50

19. B — Human resources

The human resources frame emphasizes the relationship between organizational needs and people’s needs, skills, and motivation. Role changes can also involve structure, but the decisive concern here is the fit and well-being of people.

Chapter 2 · Human resources frame · printed pp. 51–53
