Explained answer key

Page references are printed textbook pages. In the supplied PDF viewer, add 25. Scenarios and numerical examples are original unless explicitly identified as the JWD textbook case.

1. D — The implementation is a project; recurring registration processing is operations.

A project is a temporary effort that creates a unique product, service, or result. The implementation has a defined completion; routine processing continues as operations. Using technology does not determine the classification.

Chapter 1 · Projects and operations · printed pp. 4–7

2. A — Progressive elaboration

Progressive elaboration means developing a project in steps and increasing detail as understanding improves. It is not permission to add uncontrolled scope; proposed changes still need appropriate management.

Chapter 1 · Project attributes · printed pp. 6–7

3. A — Assess the effects on scope, time, cost, and other objectives, then discuss feasible trade-offs.

Scope, time, and cost interact, and quality, risk, and resources also matter. A requested scope increase requires impact analysis and a decision about trade-offs, not an unsupported promise that every objective can stay fixed.

Chapter 1 · Project constraints · printed pp. 7–9

4. D — All four groups

Stakeholders include people involved in or affected by project activities. Suppliers and opponents can influence outcomes and must not be excluded merely because they are external or unsupportive.

Chapter 1 · Stakeholders and sponsors · printed pp. 9–11

5. C — Project integration management

Integration coordinates the other knowledge areas throughout the project. Communications helps exchange information, but the decisive feature here is coordinating interdependent project decisions.

Chapter 1 · Project management knowledge areas · printed pp. 10–12

6. D — Work breakdown structure — organize the project’s work into a hierarchy

A WBS organizes the total scope hierarchically. A Gantt chart displays schedule information; a charter provides formal authorization; a weighted scoring model compares alternatives against weighted criteria.

Chapter 1 · Tools and techniques · printed pp. 12–15

7. C — It met important delivery targets, but overall success also requires considering customer satisfaction and results.

The chapter presents several ways to judge success: delivery objectives, customer or sponsor satisfaction, and achievement of the main goals or business results. Meeting constraints alone does not settle every measure of success.

Chapter 1 · Project success · printed pp. 15–18

8. B — Program management; portfolio management

Programs coordinate related projects for benefits and control that separate management would not provide. Portfolios organize and prioritize investments to serve strategy; their projects need not be directly related.

Chapter 1 · Projects, programs, and portfolios · printed pp. 18–23

9. D — Organizational project management

Organizational project management aligns project, program, and portfolio management with organizational strategy. It is broader than improving the delivery processes of a single project.

Chapter 1 · Organizational project management · printed pp. 21–23

10. C — Strategic and business management

The ninth edition’s talent triangle consists of technical project management, leadership, and strategic and business management. Understanding strategy and business value belongs to the third area; newer labels should not silently replace this edition’s terminology.

Chapter 1 · Project manager skills · printed pp. 24–29

11. D — Servant leadership

Servant leadership puts service and others’ development first. Transactional leadership emphasizes exchanges such as rewards; laissez-faire is hands-off; charismatic leadership relies heavily on personal energy and influence.

Chapter 1 · Leadership · printed pp. 27–29

12. C — Correct the report and communicate the delay honestly to the appropriate stakeholders.

PMI’s ethical values include responsibility, respect, fairness, and honesty. Reporting an anticipated recovery as an accomplished result misrepresents current performance. Ethical reporting supports informed decisions.

Chapter 1 · Professional ethics · printed pp. 34–37

13. A — True

Temporary describes the project effort, not necessarily the lifespan of its result. Building a long-lived system or facility can still be a project.

Chapter 1 · Temporary projects · printed pp. 4–7

14. B — False

The sponsor typically provides direction and funding support, while the project manager leads the work of meeting project objectives. These are distinct roles even if a particular organization sometimes combines responsibilities.

Chapter 1 · Project sponsorship · printed pp. 6–7, 9–11

15. A — True

The chapter traces the development of the profession and describes PMI and its certifications. Membership totals, salary figures, and certification details in the book are historical snapshots, not current guarantees.

Chapter 1 · Professional development · printed pp. 30–35

16. B — False

Software supports planning, analysis, and communication. People still have to define the right work, make sound assumptions, exercise judgment, and lead stakeholders; the tool cannot make those responsibilities disappear.

Chapter 1 · Project management software · printed pp. 37–39

17. D — Systems management

Systems management addresses business, technological, and organizational issues associated with creating, maintaining, or modifying systems. Systems analysis is the problem-solving approach that breaks a system into components and evaluates alternatives.

Chapter 2 · Systems approach and the three spheres · printed pp. 49–50

18. C — Organization
